#### Efficiency and effectiveness
Effectiveness = doing the right things (direction). Efficiency = doing things right (speed).
- Thrive: driving fast to the right place
- Survive: driving slow to the right place
- Die quickly: driving fast to the wrong place
- Die slowly: driving slow to the wrong place

Purpose is about direction first - effectiveness over efficiency.


## Projecting - Why should we do it?


![[Purpose-slides.pdf#page=11&rect=26,41,951,504|Purpose-slides, p.11]]

#### Vision
The answer to: Why this project? Why this goal? The link to strategy, business, economy, society and science.
Fx JFK: "before this decade is out, of landing a man on the Moon and returning him safely to the Earth."
Fx London 2012: to host an inspirational, safe and inclusive Games and leave a sustainable legacy for London and the UK.

#### The golden circle
- Why → Vision: why is the project important, what greater problem does it address?
- How → Scope: how will the project be undertaken, what are the objectives / success criteria?
- What → Deliverables: what shall the project create?

#### Goal hierarchy
Purpose → goals → deliverables, linked to the organisation's strategy and the purpose of other stakeholders. Iterative process: ask why, then what and how, then why again - until purpose, scope and requirements are clear.

#### Scoping: goals-oriented vs work-oriented
Goals-oriented: makes the purpose more concrete without limiting flexibility, delegates ownership and motivates, helps create a shared view among stakeholders.
Work-oriented: fit for routine tasks or low uncertainty, useful for unexperienced teams - but good practice is to develop the work breakdown together with the people responsible for the work.

#### SMART goals
![[Purpose-slides.pdf#page=12&rect=579,278,952,440|Purpose-slides, p.12]]
Answer to: How can the project contribute to the purpose? 
Not activity - but result 
Characteristics: Specific, Measurable, Attainable, Relevant, Time-bound 
Success criteria: How is performance going to be measured? Relating purpose and goal


#### FAST goals
![[Purpose-slides.pdf#page=13&rect=30,28,398,458|Purpose-slides, p.13]]
According to conventional wisdom, goals should be specific, measurable, achievable, realistic, and timebound. But SMART goals undervalue ambition, focus narrowly on individual performance, and ignore the importance of discussing goals throughout the year.

To drive strategy execution, leaders should instead set goals that are FAST - Frequently discussed, Ambitious, Specific, and Transparent.


#### The paradox of project planning
Important decisions are made at the start, when you have the least knowledge and information. Knowledge grows over the life cycle while the freedom to decide shrinks.

Two ways to handle it:
- Frontloading: include as much information as possible at the start of the project (analysis, prototyping, past experience)
- Postpone important decisions until you have more information (fx rolling wave planning)

#### The Rolling Wave
![[Purpose-slides.pdf#page=15&rect=65,31,928,426|Purpose-slides, p.15]]
Key idea: don't detail the entire scope up front. Plan the near future in detail and keep the rest as big blocks, which are detailed as the wave rolls forward. Industry and project-type checklists help make sure large parts have not been forgotten. Fx the Last Planner System, common in construction.

#### Scope according to ISO21502

The purpose of scope management is to facilitate the creation of the deliverables, outputs and outcomes to achieve the stated objectives of the sponsoring organization or customer. Scope management enables only formally approved work being incorporated into the project.

Activities: 
- 7.4.2 Defining the scope 
- 7.4.3 Controlling the scope 
- 7.4.4 Confirming scope delivery




## Impacting - How do we reap the benefits

- An output is any of the project's specialist products (whether tangible or intangible) 
- An outcome is the result of the change derived from using the project's outputs 
- A benefit is the measurable improvement resulting from an outcome that is perceived as an advantage by one or more stakeholders.

Output: New sales system 
Outcome: Sales orders are processed more quickly and accurately 
Benefits: Costs are reduced by 10%, volume of sales orders increased by 15% and revenue increased by 10% annually.

The chain runs output → outcome → benefit. Outputs are delivered by the project; outcomes arise when the outputs are used; benefits are the measurable improvements. We develop outputs with an eye on the benefits - this gives direction and helps prioritize.


#### Benefits and outcomes according to ISO21502

The purpose of managing organizational and societal change is to enable the project's desired outcomes to be delivered.
Activities: 
- 7.14.2 Identifying the need for change 
- 7.14.3 Implementing the organizational and societal change



The purpose of benefit management is to assist the sponsoring organization and the customer in realizing the desired benefits of a project from the project's outcomes, as described in the project's business case or other similar documentation.
Activities: 
- 7.3.2 Identifying and analysing benefits 
- 7.3.3 Monitoring benefits 
- 7.3.4 Maintaining benefits

Benefits can be realized during the project, at its end, or after closure. Before the project closes, responsibility for realizing future benefits should be transferred to the stakeholders who will own them (ISO 21502).


#### Categorization of benefits

Tangible or Intangible 
- Tangible benefits are measurable based on evidence i.e. a company may execute improvement efforts to directly reduce costs to provide a certain product. 
- Intangible benefits are not measurable objectively as they need a substituting metric to represent them i.e. customer satisfaction or a company's image. 

Planned or Emergent 
- Planned benefits are also referred to as anticipated or expected benefits meaning that they are intended gains chosen and approved. 
- Emergent benefits are unexpected benefits that may emerge across the lifecycle of a project. 

Direct or Indirect Benefits 
- Direct benefits can be measured. They are the result of realized benefits as defined before the start of a project 
- Indirect benefits are derived from direct benefits. They may occur both planned or unplanned.

#### Global impact - SDGs
Convert and integrate the project's impact to relevant SDGs, using the SDG goals and their indicators. Fx SDG 11.2 (access to safe, affordable and sustainable transport for all) with indicator 11.2.1 (proportion of the population with convenient access to public transport).



## Situating

Need to know the context in which you are operating.

#### Context according to the ISO standard
![[Purpose-slides.pdf#page=34&rect=38,30,612,448|Purpose-slides, p.34]]

Factors outside the organization: socio-economic, geographical, political, regulatory, technological and ecological. They impose requirements and constraints and introduce risks - often beyond the sponsor's or PM's control, but they must still be considered and planned for (ISO 21502, 4.2).

Organizational context (inside): the link to strategy (are projects used to execute strategy?), how projects are resourced and PMs empowered, and the organizational culture.

![[Purpose-slides.pdf#page=37&rect=35,41,720,391|Purpose-slides, p.37]]

Functional organizations are usually good for standardized and repetitive processes. Fx healthcare.
Project organizations are good at new things and projects, innovation.
![[Purpose-slides.pdf#page=38&rect=40,36,736,404|Purpose-slides, p.38]]

#### Organizational forms
Spectrum from functional over matrix (weak/balanced/strong) to projectized. The underlying design question is power: who gets it - the line or the project?
- Functional: resources sit in departments, deep specialist knowledge, clear career paths - but slow cross-functional coordination, and the PM has limited authority and is only part-time on the project.
- Matrix: combines departments and projects. The PM decides what and when, the line manager decides who and how. Risk: dual reporting and conflicting interests.
- Projectized: PM has full authority and full-time resources - good for innovation, but resources are duplicated and specialists lack a home when the project dies.

Consequence: PMs often have the responsibility to deliver but no authority over people and resources.



## Living - How should we live it?

Projects are meant to die, so they have a life cycle. How should this life cycle be organized?

![[Purpose-slides.pdf#page=48&rect=33,45,951,508|Purpose-slides, p.48]]

#### Project life cycle according to ISO21502
Project life cycle = a "defined set of phases from the start to the end of a project" (ISO 21502).
- Spans the period from the start of the project to its end
- The phases are divided by decision points (gates), which can vary depending on the organizational environment
- The decision points facilitate project governance
- By the end of the last phase, the project should have provided all deliverables

#### The fuzzy front end
When does the project actually start and end? Debate: does project management cover the front end (the definitional, development stages) or only execution and delivery? Strategic direction is set before initiation, and most benefits are realized after close-up - both sit in the standing organisation. So the scope of PM is broadened: front end → plan, execute and control, close-up → benefit realisation.

#### Project life cycle types and areas of implementation
![[Purpose-slides.pdf#page=50&rect=27,37,942,455|Purpose-slides, p.50]]

Many PLC models, mainly from the software industry: sequential, waterfall, parallel, cyclical, spiral, incremental, iterative, agile, stage-gate, code and fix.

Two families:

| | Predictive | Adaptive |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Models | Sequential, waterfall, stage-gate, (concurrent) | Cyclical, spiral, agile, iterative, code and fix |
| Foci | Discipline, controllability, linearity (speed if concurrent) | Learning, flexibility, change |
| Detailed planning | At the beginning of the project OR rolling wave | Only for each phase or iteration |
| When used | Outcome well understood, large and complex projects, need for fast completion | Outcome not well understood, rapidly changing environments |
| Customer/user involvement | Beginning, at scope changes, and project end | Continuous |

The models are not mutually exclusive - they can be combined, fx the Last Planner System (long-term predictive + short-term adaptive).
Stage-gate: phases separated by go/kill gates - the classic predictive governance model.
Agile manifesto values: individuals and interactions over processes and tools; working solutions over comprehensive documentation; customer collaboration over contract negotiation; responding to change over following a plan.



## Investing - Should we do it?

The business case: the justification for undertaking a project, in terms of evaluating the benefits, cost and risk of alternative options and the rationale for the preferred solution. Its purpose is to obtain management commitment and approval for investment in the project. The business case is owned by the sponsor.

This assumes 
- The benefits, costs and risks can be assessed 
- There is a well-understood problem that needs a solution 
- There is a defined sponsor


#### Financial appraisal

###### Payback
Compare the income that will be generated with the initial investment to determine the payback period. How long until the project is paid back.
Fx: initial investment £30m, generated revenue £6m/year → payback = 30/6 = 5 years. Companies and industries set different acceptable payback periods.

Method is simple but:
- Ignores total life-cycle costs
- Only considers costs within the payback period
- Time value of money ignored

###### Discounted cash flow

Comparison of the value of the return to the value of the sum invested had it been deposited in the bank at a given rate of interest (the opportunity cost).

Present value (PV): future value considered in today's terms.
$PV = \frac{C_{n}}{(1+i)^n}$
$C_{n}$  = future value
i = interest rate
n = years

The PV of a cash flow that happens now (n = 0) equals its face value.

Net present value:
$NPV = \text{PV of benefits}- \text{PV of costs}$
When benefits/costs occur over multiple years, sum the PVs across the years. If both occur in the same year, then $C_n = \text{benefits}_n - \text{costs}_n$.

Fx the same numbers as payback: i = 10%, £6m/year for 5 years vs £30m invested now:
$NPV = \sum_{n=1}^{5} \frac{6\text{m}}{1.1^n} - 30\text{m} \approx 22.74\text{m} - 30\text{m} = -7.26\text{m}$
Negative NPV → reject. Note the contrast: payback said 5 years and looked fine, DCF shows the project destroys value. This is why the time value of money matters.

Minimum criteria for project selection is NPV equal to or greater than 0 at a given discounting rate.


###### Internal rate of return (IRR)

The IRR is the discount rate for which the NPV (Net Present Value) is zero.

Calculated either through a
- a number of iterations with a variety of discount rates gradually getting to NPV = 0
- by graph

The higher the IRR, the more desirable the project is.
If the IRR is higher than the cost of capital, the project should be pursued.

![[Purpose-slides.pdf#page=65&rect=520,70,933,427|Purpose-slides, p.65]]

In practice, decisions don't always follow the numbers: in Danish infrastructure, projects with IRR of 19-26% were rejected while Letbanen på Ring 3 (negative IRR) was approved. The business case is also a political document.


#### Discounted cash flow/present value
Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) is a financial method that lets you take into account that time has an influence on the value of money, where money available now is worth more than money available in the future (i.e. a future cash flow). To symbolise this loss of value, future cash flows are discounted based on an interest discount rate and the number of years in the future when this cash flow happens. This projection of the value that a future cash flow has in the present is called "present value" (PV).

Fx an expected cash flow of 1m DKK in 3 years, with a yearly interest rate of 3%: $PV = \frac{1{,}000{,}000}{1.03^3} \approx 915{,}142$ DKK.

![[Purpose-slides.pdf#page=69&rect=87,70,898,285|Purpose-slides, p.69]]

![[Purpose-slides.pdf#page=70&rect=27,41,885,391|Purpose-slides, p.70]]

Takeaway from the bidding example: an initial project can be carried out at cost (or even negative NPV) to win the client, with the money made in follow-up projects. Always look at the NPV of the whole engagement.



## Evaluating - Did we succeed?

#### The magic triangle
Also called the iron triangle or triple constraints: cost, time, quality. The classic yardstick for project (management) success. Broadened version: time, cost, quality + scope and resources - and consider priorities between them.

#### Reality check
- 98% of projects incur cost overruns or delays
- The average cost increase is 80% of original value
- The average slippage is 20 months behind original schedule

Cobb's paradox: projects fail, we know why they fail, yet we continue to fail.

#### Complementary explanations
1. We need to improve the way we manage projects. True - PM is a profession now, no longer an 'accidental' one - but this alone does not explain the problem.
2. We need to change the way we measure success:
- Error type I - wrong measurements: the iron triangle is incomplete (where are safety, benefits?) and measures the wrong things - projects exist to add value to the client/society, not to be delivered on time, cost and quality.
- Error type II - measuring wrongly: cost and time baselines are often unrealistic (often deliberately!) and set when uncertainty is at its highest; quality/scope cannot be defined in detail up front, some flexibility is needed.

Classic view → state-of-the-art view: budget → business case; time, cost, scope → priorities (which success criteria matter most?); requirements → vision; short-term output → long-term benefits. Both are relevant, but efficient delivery of the wrong vision is worse than ineffective delivery of the right one.

#### Anchoring
London 2012: initial anchor ~£2.3bn even though Beijing (2008) cost £9.8bn and Barcelona (1992) £8.06bn. Estimates then climbed 1.8 → 3.14 → 4.21 → 9.3 → 11+ £bn. Optimistic anchors ignore relevant learning from past projects.

#### Sydney vs Oslo opera house
Sydney: estimated 1957 at $7m, done January 1963. Actually completed 1973 at $102m - 15x cost overrun, 10 years late. Today the symbol of Sydney and Australia, UNESCO World Heritage 2007. Success or failure?
Oslo: $52m under budget and ahead of schedule - thanks to front-end management and quality at entry.

#### Project success vs project management success
- Project management success = efficiency: deliver requirements on time, within budget.
- Project success = effectiveness: the value of the project and its long-term benefits.

Sydney = PM failure but (arguably) project success. The two are related but independent - and since benefits are realized after the project ends, success is often judged before it can really be known.

#### Managing strategic choices
Instead of juggling all three constraints, give explicit priority to one or two (time priority, cost priority, quality priority, time AND quality). Prioritisation helps the team make quick decisions and focus effort on what is most relevant.

Conformance vs performance:
- Time: as planned (can delivery be guaranteed on time?) vs shortest possible
- Cost: as budgeted vs cheapest possible
- Scope/quality: as specified vs highest achievable level

#### Cases
- Super hospitals: +6-9 months delay (Hillerød +4 years), and budget pressure led to quality reductions worth hundreds of MDKK (beds, labs, floors, patient hotel...)
- Health platform (Sundhedsplatformen): on time and on budget - but a US system incompatible with Danish healthcare, superficially implemented → substantial productivity drop for years; the business case was TOO optimistic
- IC4: delayed +7 years, 4.7 → 6.8 BDKK (+44%), long list of quality defects; completed by the client (DSB)
- Operation Warp Speed: goal of 300m doses and 20m vaccinated by end of 2020; by 30 Dec, 11.45m doses distributed but only 2.13m people vaccinated
- Eurovision venue 2014: on time, but 34.4 → 112 MDKK (+324%), quality problems (heat, dust, leaking roof), dismantled afterwards

Point: on time + on budget ≠ success (health platform), and late + expensive ≠ failure (Sydney). Judge against purpose, outcomes and benefits.



## Connecting the dots

#### Characteristics of a good purpose
1. Desirable (acceptable for key stakeholders)
- It should be inspiring and motivating
- It should address a real need or a shared strong belief
- What value would you like to create - and valuable to whom? Value is NOT a product!
2. Realistic yet ambitious
- Ambitious goals met 80% beat narrow goals developed out of fear. "Make unreasonable demands but keep sanctions fair"
3. Specific yet holds many possible solutions
- The world changes and interests are ambiguous, yet the purpose should not change during the project
- Specification sets clear direction and helps decision making - deciding what the purpose is NOT is just as important
- Implications for motivation
