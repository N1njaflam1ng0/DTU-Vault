#### The two ends of the spectrum
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=6&rect=28,60,930,470|People-slides, p.6]]
The agenda runs from the social context towards the individual: Engaging (stakeholders) → Teaming → Communicating → Mediating → Motivating → Leading. The first topics are about the world around the project, the last ones about the people in it - and about yourself.

Where Purpose deals with *why* and *what*, People deals with *who* and *whom*.



## Engaging - For whom are we doing it?

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=8&rect=28,60,930,470|People-slides, p.8]]
Purpose is developed with others, in particular the client and the user.

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=9&rect=25,35,940,505|People-slides, p.9]]
Two failure modes sit next to each other on the slide: "I know exactly what I need to do - it is all written down in the contract!" and "All indicators are green, but the client isn't satisfied!" (Maylor et al, 2009).

Something is only valuable if it is valued by the stakeholders, or as OGC puts it: benefits are only such if one or more important stakeholders consider it a benefit. Thus: listen and engage.

#### Definitions of a stakeholder
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=10&rect=25,60,940,480|People-slides, p.10]]
- Any person or organisation who can be positively or negatively impacted by, or cause an impact on, the actions of a company (Freeman, 1984)
- Person or organization actively involved in the project, or whose interests may be positively or negatively affected by execution or completion. A stakeholder may also exert influence over the project and its deliverables (PMBoK)
- Person, group or organization that has interests in, or can affect, be affected by, or *perceive itself to be affected by*, any aspect of the project, programme or portfolio (ISO 21500 & ISO 21502)

Note the ISO wording: perceiving yourself as affected is enough to make you a stakeholder. You don't get to decide who is affected.

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=11&rect=25,35,940,505|People-slides, p.11]]
Shareholder vs stakeholder: a shareholder legally owns a share of stock; a stakeholder has a stake in the project. Shareholders are a *type* of stakeholder, not the other way around.

#### Why stakeholder management matters
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=12&rect=25,35,940,505|People-slides, p.12]]
The tree-swing cartoon: how the customer explained it, how the project leader understood it, how the architects designed it, how the engineers calculated it, how the consultants described it, how it was documented, how it was built, how the customer was billed, how it was maintained - and what the customer actually needed.

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=13&rect=25,60,940,480|People-slides, p.13]]
Stakeholders are essential to project success - engagement is a must, and all parts must be kept informed. Not all stakeholders have the same importance, and importance shifts over the phases of the project.

Stakeholder management is the proactive management of reactions to the project: gaining allies and influencing potential blockers in a managed, structured fashion. It is about people - we are not managing *something*, we are managing *someone*.

#### Generic process
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=14&rect=25,60,940,480|People-slides, p.14]]
1. Identification of stakeholders
2. Assessment of stakeholders (analysis)
3. Managing the stakeholders

Key mistake: stopping in the identification phase. Stakeholder management is integral to project management - it runs until the project is complete.

#### Identifying stakeholders
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=15&rect=25,50,940,490|People-slides, p.15]]
Guiding questions (World Bank, 1996):
- Who might be affected, positively or negatively?
- Who are the 'voiceless', for whom special efforts may have to be made? Who represents those likely to be affected?
- Who is responsible for what is intended?
- Who is likely to mobilise for or against it?
- Who can make it more effective through participation - or less effective through non-participation or outright opposition?
- Who can contribute financial and technical resources?
- Whose behaviour has to change for the effort to succeed?

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=18&rect=25,50,940,490|People-slides, p.18]]
Define boundaries: too broad and you cannot manage them well enough or never reach consensus; too narrow and you miss relevant stakeholders. Usual grouping is internal/external, or as Maylor (2010) puts it: internal team, core externals and 'the rest of the world'.

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=16&rect=25,50,940,490|People-slides, p.16]]
Another way is by direction: downward, upward, outward.

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=17&rect=25,45,940,495|People-slides, p.17]]
Examples of key stakeholders (PMI, 2013): sponsor, governance board, project manager, team members, funding organization, performing organization, PMO, customers, *potential* customers, suppliers, regulatory agencies, competitors, affected individuals and organizations, and interest groups.

#### Mapping and assessment
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=19&rect=25,60,940,480|People-slides, p.19]]
Several techniques exist for categorising stakeholders. The point of all of them: identify who may support or oppose, who is most powerful or has most influence, and prioritise strategies accordingly.

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=20&rect=200,60,760,470|People-slides, p.20]]
The interest/influence matrix is the classic.

#### Tools: identification and categorization
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=22&rect=25,60,940,470|People-slides, p.22]]
Simple version - one row per stakeholder: area of interest, contribution, expectations, influence/interest.

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=23&rect=25,60,940,470|People-slides, p.23]]
Benefits version: name, category, benefits, dis-benefits, role and contribution, engagement plan, relationship.

#### Stakeholders according to ISO21502
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=21&rect=25,60,940,480|People-slides, p.21]]
The purpose of stakeholder engagement is to enable the needs, interests and concerns of stakeholders to be identified, understood and addressed sufficiently to enable the objectives to be met.

Activities:
- 7.12.2 Identifying stakeholders
- 7.12.3 Engaging stakeholders

## Teaming - Who is doing it?

#### Group vs team
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=28&rect=25,35,940,505|People-slides, p.28]]
Groups: independent individuals who interact and exchange information to achieve a particular goal. It is very clear who did what, and the result is the sum of the work of each individual.
Teams: individuals are interdependent, the work needs joint effort, and synergy effects emerge.

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=29&rect=25,60,940,480|People-slides, p.29]]

| | Groups | Teams |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Goal | Share information | Collective performance |
| Synergy | Neutral (sometimes negative) | Positive |
| Accountability | Individual | Individual and mutual |
| Skills | Random and varied | Complementary |

Important: groups and teams serve different goals - not 'good' vs 'bad', but appropriate. Ask which one you actually need before you try to build the other.

#### Dysfunctional vs effective teams
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=31&rect=25,60,940,480|People-slides, p.31]]
Dysfunctional: lack of trust, fear of conflict, lack of commitment, avoidance of accountability, inattention to results, personality clashes, groupthink.
Effective: trust, healthy conflict, commitment, accountability, results orientation, people get along well.

Note the mirror: the opposite of a dysfunctional team is not the absence of conflict but *healthy* conflict.

#### What makes teams effective
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=32&rect=30,40,935,500|People-slides, p.32]]
Four groups of factors:
- Composition: ability/competences, personality, roles, diversity, size, norms, cohesiveness, status
- Work design: autonomy, skill variety, task identity, task significance
- Context: adequate resources, leadership, climate of trust, performance evaluation and rewards
- Process: attention to the development process, common purpose, specific goals, team efficacy, conflict, social loafing

#### Cohesiveness
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=33&rect=30,40,935,470|People-slides, p.33]]
*Extra material.* Cohesiveness is the degree to which members are attracted to each other and are motivated to stay in the group. Cohesiveness drives performance - **but only with adequate work norms**. High cohesiveness with low performance norms gives low productivity; the cohesion just makes the underperformance stick.

How to build it: smaller teams, encourage agreement, increase time spent together, raise team status and the perceived difficulty of the goals, reward teams rather than individuals, physically co-locate (isolate) the team.

#### Diversity
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=34&rect=25,50,940,490|People-slides, p.34]]
*Extra material.* Advantages: different perspectives create innovative ideas and widen the range of possibilities; surface-level diversity (ethnicity, gender) encourages the search for deep-level diversity (values, perspectives); it reduces groupthink; and globalisation makes homogeneous groups hard to create anyway.
Disadvantages: conflict, miscommunication, and time spent discussing differences and reaching consensus.

Diversity pays off if the task demands it and if the team can overcome the conflicts.

#### Size
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=35&rect=25,60,940,470|People-slides, p.35]]
*Extra material.* No fixed number, but a proxy: groups needing diversity of input and fact-finding can go above 12, as long as interdependence is minimised. Teams that must create solutions and make things happen should be 5-7. If many people must be involved, create sub-teams.

#### Social loafing
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=36&rect=25,60,940,470|People-slides, p.36]]
*Extra material.* The free riders - common and human. Counter-measures: develop a common purpose and get buy-in, increase intergroup competition, peer evaluation, give people a fair share of work, make individual effort visible and distribute rewards accordingly.

#### Roles
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=37&rect=25,50,940,490|People-slides, p.37]]
*Extra material.* "All the world's a stage, and all the men and women merely players" (Shakespeare). A role is a set of expected behaviour patterns attributed to someone occupying a given position in a social unit (Robbins and Judge, 2010). Role conflict: complying with one role's requirements makes it difficult to comply with another.

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=38&rect=25,50,940,490|People-slides, p.38]]
- Role identity: behaviour becomes consistent with the role once the person assumes it - Zimbardo's prison experiment is the extreme case
- Role perceptions (how *I* think I should act) vs role expectations (how *others* think I should act). Aligning the two is the psychological contract

#### Designing the team
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=39&rect=25,60,940,480|People-slides, p.39]]
Who is needed and are they available - skills/professions/disciplines, competencies and expertise, personality types (MBTI, Whole Brain, IQ/EQ/MQ, Big 5), group dynamics and roles (Belbin). And: how and when are they needed?

#### Belbin team roles
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=40&rect=30,40,935,470|People-slides, p.40]]
Every role has a contribution *and* an allowable weakness - the weakness is the price of the strength, not a defect to be trained away. One person usually takes more than one role, and the roles describe behaviour in the team rather than profession.

- **Implementer (Company Worker)** (p.41) - conservative, dutiful, predictable. Organising ability, practical common sense, hard working, self-disciplined. Inflexible, slow to respond to new possibilities.
- **Coordinator (Chair)** (p.42) - calm, self-confident, controlled. Welcomes all contributors on their merits without prejudice, strong sense of objectives. Not especially intellectual or creative.
- **Shaper (Team Leader)** (p.43) - highly strung, outgoing, dynamic. Drive and readiness to challenge ineffectiveness and complacency. Prone to provocation, irritation and impatience.
- **Plant (Innovator)** (p.44) - individualistic, serious minded, unorthodox. Genius, imagination, intellect, knowledge. Disregards practical details, "up in the clouds", poor communicator.
- **Resource Investigator** (p.45) - extrovert, enthusiastic, curious, communicative. Makes good use of contacts, explores new ideas, responsive to challenges. Loses interest after the initial fascination passes.
- **Evaluator (Monitor)** (p.46) - sober, unemotional, prudent. Judgement, discretion, hard-headedness. Lacks inspiration and the ability to motivate others.
- **Team Worker** (p.47) - socially oriented, mild, sensitive. Responds to people and situations, promotes team spirit. Indecisive at moments of crisis.
- **Completer (Finisher)** (p.48) - painstaking, orderly, conscientious, anxious. Capacity for follow-through, perfectionism. Worries about small things, reluctant to "let go".
- **Specialist** (p.49) - not in Belbin's original classification. Single minded, self-starting, dedicated, provides rare knowledge and skills. Contributes only on a narrow front, dwells on technicalities, ignores the big picture.

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=50&rect=30,40,935,470|People-slides, p.50]]
Grouped by orientation:
- Action/task: Shaper, Implementer, Completer Finisher
- People: Coordinator, Team Worker, Resource Investigator
- Thought: Plant, Evaluator, Specialist

#### Competencies according to ISO21502
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=51&rect=30,40,935,490|People-slides, p.51]]
Three families:
- **Technical / practice**: directing, managing, planning and delivering a project in a structured way - project design, requirements and objectives, scope, time, organisation and information, quality, finance, resources, procurement, plan and control, risk and opportunity, stakeholders, change and transformation
- **Behavioural / people**: personal relationships - self-reflection and self-management, personal integrity and reliability, personal communication, relationships and engagement, leadership, teamwork, conflict and crisis, resourcefulness, negotiation, results orientation
- **Business / perspective**: managing the project within the organizational, contractual and external environment - strategy, governance, structure & processes, compliance, standards & regulation, power & interest, culture and values

#### Personality: the Big Five (OCEAN)
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=53&rect=25,60,940,480|People-slides, p.53]]
- Openness - curious, imaginative, artistic, sensitive
- Conscientiousness - responsible, dependable, persistent, organized
- Extroversion - sociable, gregarious, assertive
- Agreeableness - good-natured, cooperative, trusting
- Neuroticism / Emotional stability - calm, self-confident and secure under stress (positive) vs nervous, depressed and insecure under stress (negative)

#### Personality: MBTI
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=54&rect=25,60,940,480|People-slides, p.54]]
Built on Jung's theory that we prefer to use our mind in one of two ways across four categories; turned into an instrument by Katharine Briggs and Isabel Briggs Myers around 1940. Four dimensions, 16 types.

Caveats stated on the slide: the type does not predict job performance, your type may change over time, and - as with all psychological tests - validity is debated.

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=55&rect=25,50,940,490|People-slides, p.55]]

| Scale | | |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Energizing | Extraversion (E): energy from the outside world of people, activities, things | Introversion (I): energy from the internal world of ideas, emotions, impressions |
| Perceiving | Sensing (S): information through the five senses, what is actual | Intuition (N): information through a "sixth sense", what might be |
| Deciding | Thinking (T): decide logically and objectively | Feeling (F): decide in a personal, value-oriented way |
| Living | Judging (J): planned and organized life | Perceiving (P): spontaneous and flexible life |

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=56&rect=25,35,940,505|People-slides, p.56]]
The 16 types with population shares - note how uneven they are: ESTJ and ESFJ at 14% each, the introverted intuitives (INFJ, INTJ, INFP, INTP) at 3% each.

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=57&rect=25,60,940,470|People-slides, p.57]]
Use as a PM: understand yourself and your preferences, understand your compatibility and friction points, get the right mix of types in the team, and take advantage of the diversity of types.

#### Team development
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=58&rect=25,35,940,505|People-slides, p.58]]
Tuckman's phases, with what the PM should do in each:

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=59&rect=30,40,935,470|People-slides, p.59]]
**Forming** - the honeymoon. Get to know one another. PM clarifies purpose and goals, runs teambuilding, involves the team in planning, sets ground rules.

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=60&rect=30,40,935,470|People-slides, p.60]]
**Storming** - power struggles. PM handles conflicts, puts teamwork on the agenda, uses workshops and social events - and remembers to move on.

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=61&rect=30,40,935,470|People-slides, p.61]]
**Norming** - norms of the team, including social norms. PM shifts to coaching, the team adapts, focus moves to the project.

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=62&rect=30,40,935,470|People-slides, p.62]]
**Performing** - the productive phase. PM coaches and motivates, sustains the social part, follows up, celebrates milestones.

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=63&rect=30,40,935,470|People-slides, p.63]]
**Adjourning** - the departing phase. Tensions, deliveries, stress, new projects. PM becomes more visible again: celebrate, evaluate and learn.

Note that the PM's role is not constant: visible → conflict handler → coach → motivator → visible again.

## Communicating - How do they/we get to understand?

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=70&rect=25,60,940,480|People-slides, p.70]]
Around 80% of a manager's time is spent on communication. Projects are *based* on communication - it cuts across everything you do. And mistakes in communication are a key reason for project failure: failure to provide, or failure to receive, relevant and accurate information for the relevant people.

Essential but difficult.

#### The transmission model
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=72&rect=25,35,940,470|People-slides, p.72]]
*Extra material.* Adapted from Shannon/Weaver (1949): a message goes from an encoder/interpreter through a medium (email, phone call, face-to-face meeting) to a decoder/interpreter, disturbed by noise - "factors outside the communication process which interfere with or distract from the transmission and reception of the intended meaning" (Buchanan and Huczynski, 2010).

But this is only the rational and visible part of communication.

#### Common sources of noise
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=73&rect=25,50,940,490|People-slides, p.73]]
- Perception filters
- Field of experience - what do we share?
- Language (country, industry, discipline)
- Culture and the meaning of words
- Body language

#### Perceptions
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=75&rect=25,60,940,480|People-slides, p.75]]
"I said, but you didn't listen!" Perception is the process by which individuals attend to, organize, interpret and retain information from their environment (Williams, 2010). We need filters - otherwise we would be overloaded and unable to make sense of anything - but the consequence is that what is *said* is far from what is *heard* or understood.

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=76&rect=25,35,940,505|People-slides, p.76]]
The bandwidth of the senses makes the same point physically: far more comes in than can be consciously processed. (See also the selective attention video on p.74.)

#### Field of experience and language
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=77&rect=25,50,940,490|People-slides, p.77]]
You can only understand a message if you have some minimum common ground: shared experience, shared language, shared interest. In projects this means actively finding the common field of experience across the disciplines involved.

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=78&rect=25,60,940,480|People-slides, p.78]]
Communicating in a foreign language is hard - speak slowly and clearly. But "language" also means the language of a discipline, industry, clan or background.

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=79&rect=25,35,940,505|People-slides, p.79]]
Body language is not universal either. Fx the same hand gesture: "be patient" in Greece, "what do you mean?" in Italy, "it is perfect" in Egypt.

#### How to communicate
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=80&rect=25,60,940,470|People-slides, p.80]]
- Whom are you targeting?
- What is the message?
- When should it be communicated?
- Whose responsibility is it to bring it out?
- What is the desired outcome?

#### Choosing the medium
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=81&rect=25,35,940,470|People-slides, p.81]]
Oral is real-time conversation - useful in emotional, ambiguous or complex situations, fx co-creation of value and negotiation. Written is not real-time and one-way at each time - useful for straightforward information, specific tasks and sealing mutual agreements. Visualisation and video sit in between.

#### Meetings
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=82&rect=25,60,940,480|People-slides, p.82]]
Meetings are one of the most important activities in project work and form the context of very different project activities - yet little attention is given to their organisation and design. A meeting must not be a waste of time: design and facilitate it based on its purpose.

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=83&rect=25,50,940,490|People-slides, p.83]]
Three phases:
- **Before**: is the meeting necessary? Involve and invite the important and relevant people, coordinate calendars, prepare an agenda with purpose and expected deliverables.
- **During**: clear agenda, clear timeframe, clear purpose, dialogue instead of monologue, defined roles and responsibilities.
- **After**: minutes, defined deliverables, follow-up.

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=84&rect=25,50,940,490|People-slides, p.84]]

| Meeting type | Purpose | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Involvement | Create enthusiasm, motivation, interest, shared understanding and acceptance | Kick-off, information meetings |
| Ideation | Collaborate to get as many ideas as possible | Brainstorming, product and concept development |
| Problem solving | Find solution(s) to a given problem | Crisis meetings, planning meetings |
| Decision | Make decisions based on submitted/presented material | Board meetings, follow-up meetings |

#### Golden email rules
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=86&rect=25,60,940,470|People-slides, p.86]]
General: when possible, talk instead of type - around 2/3 of people hate email. Set expectations for response times (email is not a phone call and is unsuitable for urgent communication), for how many urgent emails are allowed, and for writing style. Emails are never private.

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=87&rect=25,50,940,490|People-slides, p.87]]
Writing: minimise the number of recipients; one message to convey = one email; be short and precise; proper and current subject line; be clear about purpose (information, discussion, required actions and time frame); never argue via email - sleep on emotional emails; with sensitive emails, enter the recipient's address only when you are done writing.

Receiving: never start your day by reading email; check once or twice a day with enough time to finish; only touch an email once - delete, delegate, handle immediately (5-10 minute tasks), or add to the todo list (better: schedule it in the calendar). An inbox is not a todo list.

#### Self-awareness
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=89&rect=25,35,940,470|People-slides, p.89]]
*Extra material.* A two-by-two on caring about yourself vs caring about the other:
- Assertive: I'm OK, you are OK - I care about myself *and* you
- Aggressive: I'm OK, you are not OK - I care about myself, not about you
- Passive: I'm not OK, you are OK - I care about you, not myself
- Passive-aggressive: I'm not OK, you are not OK - I care about neither

Good communication skills require a high level of self-awareness; knowing your own style is what makes lasting impressions possible.

#### Communication and information according to ISO21502
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=85&rect=25,50,940,490|People-slides, p.85]]
The purpose of communication management is to enable stakeholder interactions that are effective and likely to contribute to the successful delivery of the project's outcomes and the successful realization of benefits.
Activities:
- 7.13.2 Planning communication
- 7.13.3 Distributing information
- 7.13.4 Monitoring the impact of communications

The purpose of information and documentation management is to enable relevant and reliable information (physical and digital) to be available to those undertaking work and making decisions.
Activities:
- 7.16.2 Identifying which information should be managed
- 7.16.3 Storing and retrieving information and documentation

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=88&rect=25,35,940,505|People-slides, p.88]]

## Mediating - How do we handle conflicts?

#### Mary Parker Follett (1868-1933)
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=92&rect=25,45,940,495|People-slides, p.92]]
*Extra material.* The 'mother of management'. Concerned with productivity like Taylor, but disagreed with his methods because they devalued human creativity. Her positions:
- Conflict is inevitable in groups, but must be handled so the group can achieve 'integrity unity amongst the members' - cognitive conflict and integrative conflict resolution
- **Power with** instead of power over: giving orders means discussing the instruction and dealing with the resentment it causes
- Neither person should give the order - both should agree to take their orders from *the situation*
- Management should try to understand why and how groups form and weld together; she believed in self-governance, creativity, responsibility for results, participation in decision making, and both vertical and horizontal communication

#### Definitions of conflict
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=93&rect=25,60,940,480|People-slides, p.93]]
- An escalation of a disagreement, characterized by conflict behavior in which the people involved actively try to damage one another (Nicholson, 1992)
- An interactive process manifested in incompatibility, disagreement or dissonance within or between social entities (Rahim, 2010)

#### Unitary vs pluralist view
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=95&rect=25,50,940,490|People-slides, p.95]]
Unitary - conflicts are bad: conflicts are negative and destructive; the organization is a team working towards a common goal defined by management; rational behaviour is expected and defined by company policy.

Pluralist - conflicts can be constructive if managed: controversy is natural and constructive; organizations consist of different stakeholders with different values, views, stories and goals; conflicts emerge as a consequence of colliding differences (controversies) that are *not dealt with*.

The pluralist view is more in line with contemporary understanding of people, management and organizations.

#### Sources of conflict in projects
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=96&rect=25,50,940,490|People-slides, p.96]]
Unclear tasks, incompatible goals and time frames, lack of resources, unclear communication and misunderstanding, incompatible evaluation and award systems, different realities, overlapping authorities, differences in perceptions and values.

#### Balancing conflict and controversy
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=97&rect=25,35,940,470|People-slides, p.97]]
Too little conflict is as much a problem as too much - the middle of the curve is where performance sits (Huczynski & Buchanan 2011; Hatch 1997; Robbins 1998).

#### When controversy becomes conflict
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=99&rect=25,35,940,470|People-slides, p.99]]
An escalation ladder from controversy to conflict:
1. A discrepancy - we do not agree
2. *Threshold* - personification: it is your fault
3. The problem escalates - there is no trust any more
4. *Threshold* - dialogue is given up, we talk *about* each other rather than *to* each other
5. Enemy images, projections
6. Open hostility, demolition
7. Polarization - no coexistence

The two thresholds are the ones to watch: the moment a disagreement becomes about a person, and the moment people stop talking to each other. Intervening is cheap below a threshold and expensive above it.

#### Active listening
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=102&rect=25,60,940,480|People-slides, p.102]]
We listen to obtain information, to understand, for enjoyment, and to learn - but research suggests we remember only 25-50% of what we hear.

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=103&rect=25,60,940,470|People-slides, p.103]]
Active listening is a communication technique that increases understanding and trust between speaker and listener. Its purpose is to make the story-teller comfortable telling their story, and to make the listener understand the complete message.

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=104&rect=25,60,940,470|People-slides, p.104]]

| Just listening | Active listening |
| --- | --- |
| Your attention is elsewhere | You focus on the other person |
| You are thinking of what to say next | Curious, like a child |
| Waiting to tell your own story | Empathic, understanding |
| Interrupting | Let the person finish before you talk |

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=110&rect=25,60,940,470|People-slides, p.110]]
Four moves:
- **Pay attention** (p.105) - look at the speaker directly, put aside distracting thoughts, don't be distracted by the environment, don't mentally prepare a rebuttal, "listen" to body language. Tip: if concentration is hard, repeat their words mentally as they say them.
- **Show that you're listening** (p.106) - nod, smile, open posture, small verbal encouragements, clarifying questions. But don't interrupt and don't judge.
- **Provide feedback** (p.107) - paraphrase ("what I'm hearing is..."), ask clarifying questions, summarize periodically. Tip: if you find yourself reacting emotionally, say so and ask for more information - "what I thought you said is X; is that what you meant?"
- **Defer judgement** (p.108) - let the speaker finish each point, don't interrupt with counter-arguments, and don't be afraid of silence.

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=109&rect=25,60,940,480|People-slides, p.109]]
Then respond appropriately: be candid, open and honest; assert your opinions respectfully; treat others as you would like to be treated.

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=111&rect=25,35,940,505|People-slides, p.111]]
The collaboration model and the contractual structure between DR, the Project Company and the other host city parties did not promote collaboration, but contributed to disagreements and a focus on self-interest at the expense of the project's finances and progress.

## Motivating - What motivates us/them?

#### Historical development: scientific management
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=116&rect=25,45,940,495|People-slides, p.116]]
Taylorism, from the early 1900s, most applicable to repetitive work and responsible for a massive increase in productivity. Core belief: motivation = money. Measure, record and analyse work to determine how it should be divided for maximum efficiency; match worker to job; train for THE one most efficient way; supervise closely; reward exact conformance.

Consequences: physical work and thinking are separated, tasks are simple and repetitive (leading to proficiency), workers have no input and can become alienated - passively (don't care) or destructively (sabotage). Fx watch a burger being produced at McDonald's. (The Chaplin *Modern Times* clip on p.115 is the illustration.)

For projects: at the lowest level of the WBS some tasks are repetitive within or across projects, and productivity can be improved by spotting them (fx measuring material movement to reveal excessive distances on a construction site). For each of us: much repetitive knowledge work is now being automated - identify and automate your own 'brainless' operations.

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=117&rect=25,50,940,490|People-slides, p.117]]
Critique:
- Contributions: importance of compensation, better understanding of tasks and performance, importance of personnel selection and development, transparency in decisions and sanctions
- Criticism: ignores the social context of work and the differences between individuals; treats people as pieces in a machine with no brains, emotions or ideas; leaves no room for flexibility or innovation; cannot cope with uncertainty or change because it cannot pre-programme the solution

#### The Hawthorne studies
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=118&rect=25,45,940,495|People-slides, p.118]]
In the 1920s Elton Mayo and Fritz Roethlisberger tested the impact of working conditions (light intensity, pace control) on productivity. As expected, productivity rose when lighting increased. But it *stayed* high when lighting returned to the original level - and the control group improved too.

What this rejected and what it established:
- Physical conditions had limited impact on productivity
- Group control (peer pressure) played a major role, and incentives did not abolish it
- Private and work life were more interlinked than expected
- Financial rewards are not the only motivator - even at that time
- The measuring process affects the performance of what is being measured: 'what gets measured gets done'

This was the birth of motivation studies and a more human approach to management.

#### Theory X and Theory Y (McGregor)
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=119&rect=25,50,940,490|People-slides, p.119]]
- Theory X: average people are lazy and dislike work; they need close supervision and authoritarian managers; comprehensive control and reward systems
- Theory Y: average people do not dislike work - work may be a source of satisfaction or of punishment; people exercise self-control and self-direction; the reward is self-actualisation

The point is not which is *true* but that the assumption is self-fulfilling: it is our job - our own, our peers' and our managers' - to turn work into satisfaction.

#### Map of motivation theories
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=120&rect=25,35,940,470|People-slides, p.120]]
- **Input (needs) → content theories**: a hidden catalogue of things we want and need. We are prepared to do things or behave in certain ways to satisfy our needs.
- **Process (actions/behaviour) → process theories**: what are we prepared to do to satisfy a want or need, and to what extent do we believe this behaviour will lead to the desired outcome?
- **Outcome → reinforcement (extrinsic)** - reward or punishment given to encourage desired and discourage undesired behaviour; note the reward can come from peers, not only managers. **And intrinsic motivation** - the internal force that makes us want to achieve something.

All of it feeds work-oriented motivation: how we use the theories to design work.

#### Content theories: Maslow's hierarchy of needs
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=121&rect=25,35,940,470|People-slides, p.121]]
The slide's practical framing: family worries, job safety and health issues sit at the deficiency levels where we may have *some* influence; where we *want* people to focus is at the top.

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=123&rect=25,45,940,495|People-slides, p.123]]
"Man is a wanting animal and rarely reaches a state of complete satisfaction except for a short time. As one desire is satisfied, another pops up to take its place" (Maslow, 1943).

Principles:
1. Climb the ladder - the basics come before you can move up
2. There is an innate desire to move up
3. A satisfied need is not a motivator: more safety does not motivate someone who already has safety
4. Self-actualisation is the only need that cannot be fully satisfied
5. Unsatisfied needs can affect mental health - no self-esteem, inability to form relationships, depression
6. The first four are D-needs (deficit needs): you don't miss water until you don't have it. They are not motivational mechanisms but developmental ones; deprivation in early phases can leave an oversized need later (fx loss of a parent turning into an extra need for love and social belonging), and regressions happen when basic needs stop being met
7. Self-actualisation is a B-need (being need, growth): be all you can be, exploit your potential. Learning is central to it - this is where you develop your individual identity as a project participant

The levels: basic physiological (air, water, food) → safety and security (stability, protection - the market for insurance and private security) → social (belonging, love, affection, friendship) → self-esteem (lower: recognition, pride, power, glory, reputation; higher: self-respect, achievement, competence) → self-actualisation (growth, wisdom).

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=124&rect=25,50,940,490|People-slides, p.124]]
Criticisms:
- Different cultures order the hierarchy differently, fx social before security
- No evidence people satisfy one level at a time
- How does it explain the starving musician?
- Methodological problem - picking a few people he defined as self-actualised is not really scientific
- It does not predict behaviour
- If people cannot perform without feeling safe, how do we explain performance in today's context?

For projects: motivation can be provided by letting an individual pursue as yet unmet needs.

#### Acquired needs
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=125&rect=25,60,940,480|People-slides, p.125]]
*Extra material.* Some needs, even if innate, are heavily influenced by context - social norms, culture, past experience, phase in life. Fx what counts as status varies from culture to culture. And not all needs are innate; we acquire more throughout life. McClelland proposes three acquired needs: achievement, affiliation and power.

#### Process theories: expectancy theory
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=126&rect=25,35,940,470|People-slides, p.126]]
*Extra material.* Behaviour depends on whether we perceive that the behaviour will lead to the desired outcome. The theory assumes behaviour is entirely rational.

$MF = V \times I \times E$
- **E - Expectancy**: probability that effort will lead to performance
- **I - Instrumentality**: probability that performance will lead to the outcome
- **V - Valence**: the outcome has positive or negative value to the employee

Because the terms multiply, a zero anywhere kills the motivation - it is not enough to fix only one of them.

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=127&rect=25,40,940,500|People-slides, p.127]]
Managerial implications:
- On effort → performance: provide training, coach people with low self-confidence, select people with the required skills, clarify job requirements, provide examples and success stories, set role expectations, provide supporting systems
- On performance → outcome: measure performance accurately, explain outcomes and rewards clearly
- On outcomes: offer only rewards employees actually value - and since people value different things, provide variety (promotion, larger office, parking space, but also autonomy, inspiring or enriching work, learning opportunities), balanced against the need for a clear, transparent and fair reward system. Make sure all types of outcome are clearly linked to the desired performance. If an individual is instructed to do one thing but rewarded for another, they will ignore the instruction.

#### Outcome theories: reinforcement (extrinsic)
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=128&rect=25,45,940,495|People-slides, p.128]]
Pioneered by Skinner, the starting point of behavioural psychology. The idea: condition people by adding or removing rewarding (praise, rewards) or aversive (punishment) stimulus. Highly debated, but useful in some circumstances. What we have learned:
- Adding rewards is more effective than removing punishments
- Aversive feedback is less effective if you don't provide a way to improve (which expectancy theory explains)
- Too much reinforcement can *reduce* intrinsic motivation

Take-aways: be specific (praise specific achievements on current information); be immediate; make targets achievable (break tasks into milestones and praise each); remember the intangible (praise may motivate more than pay); make it unpredictable (a passing comment can beat the expected pat on the back).

#### Outcome theories: intrinsic motivation
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=129&rect=25,45,940,495|People-slides, p.129]]
Behaviour driven by internal rewards - you do it because you find it rewarding, independent of what others think or what external outcome you get. The internal reward is the positive emotion associated with the task: enjoying learning a new subject, reaching the top of a mountain, completing a hard task.

To encourage it in others: create tasks that build on curiosity, challenge, autonomy and the ability to help others.

For projects: intrinsic motivation is more resilient to the usual setbacks, and it is essential when creating new projects - it lets you develop an interesting and relevant purpose because you and your peers see it matters, not because someone will praise you for it. (See Dan Pink's TED talk / *Drive*, linked on p.130.)

Autonomy, mastery and purpose in practice:

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=131&rect=25,60,940,480|People-slides, p.131]]
**Autonomy**: flexible working hours, home office, self-organised and trust-based hours, flat hierarchies, open-door policy, Kaizen-type bottom-up improvement, discretionary funds, "Free Friday", responsibility-based planning and control aligning responsibility, authority and accountability.

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=132&rect=25,60,940,470|People-slides, p.132]]
**Mastery**: specialist career paths (not everyone wants to be a manager), communities of practice, mentoring, hiring for attitude and training for skill.

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=133&rect=25,60,940,470|People-slides, p.133]]
**Purpose**: a clear, strong, shared vision of the bigger-picture contribution of the project; value stream mapping; a clear "line of sight" between personal objectives, project deliverables, benefits and strategy.

Note the link back to Purpose: the goal hierarchy is not only a planning device, it is a motivational one.

#### Other individual factors
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=134&rect=25,60,940,470|People-slides, p.134]]
*Extra material.* Location (motivation systems and expectations differ in rural and urban settings), length of service (long service creates different needs from a newcomer's), new teams (early guidance and feedback define expectations), previous environment (people keep reacting according to prior constraints).

#### Motivation in short
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=135&rect=25,60,940,470|People-slides, p.135]]
Include the team in planning activities. Reward and acknowledge. Show interest in the individual. Delegate responsibility. Celebrate achievements. Show your commitment through words *and* action.

#### Case: user involvement in the design of super hospitals
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=136&rect=25,35,940,505|People-slides, p.136]]
Reflection: how were team members motivated, and how were stakeholders involved to ensure commitment and acceptance?



## Leading - How do we guide?

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=144&rect=25,60,940,470|People-slides, p.144]]
Leadership is an elusive term with several approaches and definitions. One working definition: leadership occurs among people, involves the use of influence, and is used to attain goals (Daft et al, 2010).

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=145&rect=25,60,940,480|People-slides, p.145]]
The opening questions: what makes a great leader, is that different from being a good manager, what traits do great business leaders have, and could project managers usefully adopt them? In project management the terms are often interchanged, and excellence requires elements of both.

#### Management vs leadership
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=146&rect=25,60,940,480|People-slides, p.146]]
Management plays a positive role in achieving goals; it is the technical discipline of applying and administering authority over others, and that authority is determined by the formal structure of the organisation. Drucker: the manager is the dynamic, life-giving element in every business - without leadership, the resources of production remain resources and never become production.

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=147&rect=25,50,940,490|People-slides, p.147]]
Leadership is the quality of obtaining results from others through personal influence - influencing others through personality or action. It requires the right individual skill and attitude.
- The traits approach: 'leaders are born and not made'
- Skills and attributes: teachable, provided the individual wants to learn
- Intelligence: cannot be taught, but is rarely the constraint on success
- Further research: there is no single recipe for success

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=148&rect=25,45,940,495|People-slides, p.148]]

| Leadership qualities | Manager qualities |
| --- | --- |
| Doing the right things (effectiveness) | Doing things right (efficiency) |
| Visionary, passionate, creative, flexible, inspiring, innovative, courageous, imaginative, experimental, initiates change | Rational, consulting, persistent, problem solving, tough-minded, analytical, structured, deliberate, authoritative, stabilizing |
| Personal power | Position power |

(Capowski, 1994. Note the effectiveness/efficiency split is the same one that opens the Purpose material.)

Is being a leader better than being a manager? No - the question is which the situation calls for.

#### The behaviour perspective
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=149&rect=25,35,940,470|People-slides, p.149]]
Two dimensions of behaviour:
1. **Relationship-oriented** (concern for people): mindful of subordinates, respecting their feelings and ideas; mutual trust; genuine concern; open communication; looking out for their welfare
2. **Task-oriented** (concern for task): assigning people to key tasks; clarifying duties and roles; pushing people to reach their performance capacity; establishing deadlines and plans

Plotted against each other, the grid gives impoverished management, country-club management, authority-compliance, middle-of-the-road and team management.

Take-aways: don't focus only on the leader but on the team (the followers) to understand leadership; balance task and relationship, adapt to the situation - and if you have to choose, go for relationship.

#### Situational leadership
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=151&rect=25,35,940,470|People-slides, p.151]]
Hersey & Blanchard (1969): the directing PM *informs*, the supportive PM *communicates*. The level of competence and the level of commitment of the person determine what kind of leadership is needed. Leadership style is therefore not a personality trait to settle on once, but a choice per person and per situation.

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=152&rect=25,35,940,505|People-slides, p.152]]
The MBTI grid returns here in a leadership-flavoured version - fx ENTJ as "life's natural leaders", ENFJ as the smooth-talking persuader, ESTJ as the organized pillar of strength. Reflection (p.153): what are your leadership style and preferences, task- or relationship-oriented, and how does your MBTI type influence them?

#### Followership
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=155&rect=25,35,940,505|People-slides, p.155]]
Kelley (in Daft et al, 2008) maps followers on independent/critical thinking vs active/passive engagement:
- **Effective** ("the Stars"): consistent behaviour towards everyone regardless of their power; deal well with conflict, risk and change; put forward their own views while staying focused on what the organisation needs; mindful of how others see them; make acts of leadership often and use referent, expert, network and information power in the service of the organisation
- **Alienated**: think extremely well but snipe from the sidelines; stuck, negative, feel they have lost their power; often bitter from being passed over or staying too long in one position
- **Conformist** ("the Yes-People"): very busy, participate willingly, but don't question orders; avoid conflict at all costs and take the quietest path, while defending their boss to loyal extremes
- **Passive** ("the Sheep"): neither engage their brain nor take concrete action; often the product of micro-managers or a negative, over-controlling, blame-oriented culture
- **Pragmatic survivor**: the canary in the mine shaft - flips between styles to suit the situation, and is the early warning system that the culture is starting to change for the worse

Two things follow: the passive and conformist types are largely *produced* by the leadership around them, and the pragmatic survivors are worth watching as an indicator. (See also the Derek Sivers "how to start a movement" talk on p.154 - the first follower is what turns a lone nut into a leader.)

#### To manage others, you must first manage yourself
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=156&rect=25,35,940,505|People-slides, p.156]]

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=167&rect=25,60,940,470|People-slides, p.167]]
*Extra material from here.* Individual skills and attitudes come from personality, experience, and formal and informal training. Most PM skills are learnable. Start with personal (as opposed to personnel) management - and treat time as the non-replenishable resource it is.

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=168&rect=25,50,940,490|People-slides, p.168]]
Time allocation:
- **Proactive**: looking beyond what has to be done now, problem prevention
- **Reactive**: fire-fighting - rewarding and high-stress, but progress on innovative matters becomes haphazard
- **Inactive**: resting time, which is important and should *not* include thinking time

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=169&rect=25,60,940,470|People-slides, p.169]]
Stress: pressure can be beneficial - rising to a challenge enhances performance. The negative side is stress, with symptoms from anxiety to breakdown. Plan your way out, pace yourself, reward yourself on accomplishments, and laugh.

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=170&rect=25,50,940,490|People-slides, p.170]]
Relieving it: analyse the current situation by logging time spent over several weeks with priority and comment; set short, medium and long term goals across professional, financial and personal life (SMART, and also accountable, regularly reviewed, stretching, exciting); change the plan if there is a discrepancy between objectives and time allocated; do one thing at a time and eliminate distractions; balance life with sport and recreation. (Tools on p.171-172.)

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=160&rect=25,60,940,470|People-slides, p.160]]
David Allen's *Getting Things Done*: do it, delegate it, defer it, drop it; handle email, paperwork and unexpected demands in a system of self-management; the two-minute rule for deciding what to do now and what to defer. (Diagrams on p.161-162.)

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=163&rect=25,45,940,495|People-slides, p.163]]
Stephen Covey's *7 Habits* - private victory: be proactive, begin with the end in mind, put first things first. Public victory: think win/win, seek first to understand then to be understood, synergize. And the seventh: sharpen the saw.

![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=166&rect=25,60,940,470|People-slides, p.166]]
Daniel Goleman on emotional intelligence and primal leadership.

#### Case: super hospitals
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=157&rect=25,35,940,505|People-slides, p.157]]
Changing the healthcare system eventually affects all citizens as patients, relatives and employees, so a key process has been managing the public perception of the transformation. A politician quoted on the slide: closing hospitals was a bloody process that cost politicians their re-election - he began his career elected to protect a small hospital, had to close it anyway, and then took the sign down himself when the locals asked.

## Connecting the dots

#### Key focus areas
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=174&rect=25,35,940,505|People-slides, p.174]]
Attracting, engaging, integrating - the three things you are doing with people across the whole lifecycle. Everything else is rework.

#### A word of ethics: Kant's categorical imperative
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=175&rect=25,60,940,470|People-slides, p.175]]
Always treat people as ends in themselves, never as a means to an end. Never value a person only because they are a way of achieving something else.

This is the ethical floor under everything above: stakeholder mapping, team design, motivation techniques and reinforcement are all instruments for working *with* people, and they stop being legitimate the moment people become instruments themselves.

#### The four questions of a project
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=176&rect=25,35,940,505|People-slides, p.176]]
- **Purpose**: why? what?
- **Complexity**: how? where? when?
- **Uncertainty**: what if? what now? so what?
- **People**: who? whom?

People is the fourth question, and the one the other three run through - complexity is handled by organising people, uncertainty is absorbed by people, and purpose only exists because someone values it.
