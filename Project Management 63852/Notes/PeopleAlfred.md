### Engaging: For whom are we doing it?

#### Stakeholder definitions
- Any person or organisation who can be positively or negatively impacted by, or cause an impact on the actions of a company. (Freeman, 1984)
- "Person or organization  that is actively involved in the project, or whose interests may be positively or negatively affected by execution or completion of the project. A stakeholder may also exert influence over the project and its deliverables." (PMBoK)
- Person, group or organization that has interests in, or can affect, be affected by, or perceive itself to be affected by, any aspect of the project, programme or portfolio. (ISO 21500 & ISO 21502)

Shareholder ≠ stakeholder: a shareholder legally owns stock; a stakeholder has a stake in the project. Shareholders are a *type* of stakeholder.

#### Why stakeholder management matters
"All indicators are green, but the client isn't satisfied!" (Maylor et al, 2009)

Benefits are only such if one or more important stakeholders consider it as a benefit (OGC) → LISTEN and ENGAGE.
- Stakeholders are essential to project success, engagement is a must
- Not all stakeholders have the same importance (varies per area and per phase)
- Stakeholder management is proactive management of reactions: gaining allies, influencing potential blockers
- We are not managing something, but *someone*!

#### Generic processes
1. Identification of stakeholders
2. Assessment of stakeholders (analysis)
3. Managing the stakeholders

Key mistake: stopping in the identification phase. Stakeholder management runs until the project is complete.

#### Identifying stakeholders + boundaries
Guiding questions (World Bank, 1996): who is affected, who is 'voiceless', who is responsible, who mobilises for/against, who contributes resources, whose behaviour must change.
Define boundaries: internal team, core externals and 'rest of the world' (Maylor, 2010).
- Too broad → cannot manage well enough / never reach consensus
- Too narrow → miss relevant stakeholders

#### Classifying stakeholders by direction
- Downward (project group)
- Upward (owner, steering group, directors, bosses — those who set the assignment)
- Outward (other internal stakeholders like owners, shareholders, colleagues + external stakeholders like customers, suppliers, partners, unions, competitors, authorities).

### Teaming: Who is doing it?

#### Groups vs. teams
Groups → independent individuals that interact and exchange information; result = sum of individual work.

Teams → interdependent individuals; work needs joint effort; synergy effects emerge.

| | GROUPS | TEAMS |
|---|---|---|
| Goal | Share information | Collective performance |
| Synergy | Neutral (sometimes negative) | Positive |
| Accountability | Individual | Individual and mutual |
| Skills | Random and varied | Complementary |

#### Dysfunctional vs effective teams
Dysfunctional: 
- lack of trust
- fear of conflict
- lack of commitment
- avoidance of accountability
- inattention to results
- personality clashes
- groupthink, shared understanding that is counterproductive to what you want to achieve

Effective: 
- trust
- healthy conflict
- commitment
- accountability
- results orientation
- people get along well

#### Team effectiveness model
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=32&rect=28,43,894,471|People-slides-Jan-2023, p.32]]


EXTRA: cohesiveness ↔ performance only with adequate work ethics. 
Diversity is advantageous if the task demands it. 
Teams creating solutions: 5–7 members
fact-finding groups can be >12. 
Social loafing = free riders. Counter with common purpose, peer evaluation, identifiable individual effort.

How to create cohesive teams? 
- Make smaller teams 
- Encourage agreement 
- Increase time spent together 
- Increase team status and perceived difficulty to achieve goals 
- Give rewards to teams not individuals 
- Physically isolate the teams
#### Designing the team
Who is needed and are they available? Skills, competencies, personality types (MBTI, Big 5), group dynamics/roles (Belbin). How and when are they needed?

#### Competencies according to ISO 21502
- Technical competencies: for directing, managing, planning and delivering a project in a structured way 
- Behavioural competencies: personal relationships — self-reflection and self-management, personal integrity and reliability, personal communication, relationships and engagement, leadership, teamwork, conflict and crisis, resourcefulness, negotiation, results orientation
- Business and other competencies: related to management in the organisational and wider context

#### Personality: The big five (OCEAN)
- Openness: curious, imaginative, artistic, sensitive
- Conscientiousness: responsible, dependable, persistent, organized
- Extroversion: sociable, gregarious, assertive
- Agreeableness: good-natured, cooperative, trusting
- Neuroticism / Emotional stability: calm and secure under stress (positive) vs nervous, insecure (negative)

#### Personality: MBTI
4 dimensions → 16 types. Type does not predict job performance, may change over time, validity is debated.
Energizing: 
- Extraversion, energy from the outside world of people, activities, things
- Introversion, energy from one's internal world of ideas, emotions, impressions
Perceiving: 
- Sensing, information through the five senses, what is actual
- Intuition  through a "sixth sense", what might be
Deciding: 
- Thinking, deciding in a logical, objective way
- Feeling in a personal, value-oriented way
Living: 
- Judging, planned and organized life
- Perceiving, spontaneous and flexible life

How to use MBTI as a PM: understand yourself and your preferences, understand compatibility and friction points, get the right mix of types, take advantage of diverse types.

#### Team development (Tuckman)
Five stages of team development.

Forming: 
- The honeymoon. Get to know each other. 
- PM: clarify purpose and goals, teambuilding, involve team in planning, ground rules.
Storming: 
- Power struggles. 
- PM: handle conflicts, workshops, teamwork on the agenda, social events, remember to move on.
Norming: 
- Norms of the team incl. social norms. 
- PM: coaching role, adapting, more focus on the project.
Performing: 
- The productive phase. Most time is spent here.
- PM: coach and motivate, sustain social part, follow-up, celebrate milestones.
Adjourning: 
- The departing phase; tensions, stress, new projects. 
- PM: more visible again, celebrate, evaluate and learn.

### Communicating: How do they/we get to understand?

#### Why communication matters
~80% of a manager's time is spent on communication. Communication cuts across everything in projects; mistakes in communication (failure to provide / to 'receive' relevant, accurate information to relevant people) are a key reason for project failure. Essential but difficult!
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=71&rect=14,43,951,412|People-slides-Jan-2023, p.71]]


#### Communication model
Sender (encoder) → medium (email, phone call, face-to-face) → receiver (decoder), disturbed by noise: "factors outside the communication process which interfere with or distract from the transmission and reception of the intended meaning". Both parties encode, interpret and decode.

#### Common issues
- Perception: 
	- "the process by which individuals attend to, organize, interpret, and retain information from their environment". Filters are necessary but mean what is said ≠ what is understood.
- Field of experience: 
	- one can only understand your message with minimum common ground → find shared experience, language, interest. Crucial across disciplines in projects.
- Body language: 
	- same gesture means different things across cultures.
- Language:
	- speak slowly and clearly.
	- language differs accross job, cultures, countries.

#### How to communicate
Whom are you targeting? What is the message? When should it be communicated? Whose responsibility? What is the desired outcome?

#### Communication & information according to ISO 21502
Communication management (7.13): 
- enable stakeholder interactions that are effective and likely to contribute to successful delivery of outcomes and realization of benefits. 
- Activities: 
	- 7.13.2 Planning communication. 
	- 7.13.3 Distributing information.
	- 7.13.4 Monitoring the impact of communications.

Information and documentation management (7.16): 
- enable relevant and reliable information (physical and digital) to be available to those undertaking work and making decisions. 
- Activities: 
	- 7.16.2 Identifying which information should be managed
	- 7.16.3 Storing and retrieving information and documentation.

#### Golden email rules
- When possible, talk instead of type! 
- Emails are never private. 
- One message = one email
	- short and precise
	- proper subject line
	- clear purpose (information / discussion / required actions and time frame). 
- Never argue via email — sleep over emotional emails. 
- Receiving: check only once or twice per day, touch an email only once, actions: delete / delegate / take care immediately (5–10 min tasks).

### Mediating: How do we handle conflicts?

#### Definition of conflict
- Conflict is an escalation of a disagreement, characterized by the existence of conflict behavior, in which people involved are actively trying to damage one another. (Nicholson, 1992)
- An interactive process manifested in incompatibility, disagreement or dissonance within or between social entities. (Rahim, 2010)

#### Perspectives on conflicts
Unitary view(conflicts are bad):
- Conflicts are negative and destructive
- The organization is a team where the members are exprected to work towards a common goal
- The common goal is defined by the management
- Rational behavior is expected and is defined in accordance with company policy

Pluralist view(conflicts can be constructive):
- Controversy is a natural and constructive phenomena
- Organizations conists of different stakeholders who have different values, view, stories, goals
- Conflicts emerge as a consequence of colliding differences(controversies) that are not dealt with.

Pluralist view is more contemporay view of people, organizations and management

#### Active listening
We remember only 25–50% of what we hear. 

Just listening: attention elsewhere, thinking of what to say next, waiting to tell your own story, interrupting.

Active listening is a communication technique that increases understanding and trust between speaker and listener.

Active listening: focus on the other person, curious, empathic, let the person finish.
1. Pay attention: look at the speaker, put aside distracting thoughts, don't prepare a rebuttal, "listen" to body language
2. Show that you're listening: nod, smile, open posture, small verbal comments, clarification questions — don't interrupt, don't judge
3. Provide feedback: paraphrase ("What I'm hearing is…"), ask clarifying questions, summarize periodically
4. Defer judgment: allow the speaker to finish, no counter-arguments, don't fear silence
5. Respond appropriately: candid, open, honest; assert opinions respectfully; golden rule

### Motivating: What motivates us/them?

#### Historical development
1. Scientific management (Taylorism, Frederick Taylor, early 1900s): 
	- Motivation = money. Measure, record, analyse; match worker to job; train THE one best way; closely supervise; reward exact compliance. 
	- Separates physical work and thinking → alienation (passive or destructive). 
	- Contributions: compensation, task understanding, personnel selection, transparency. 
	- Criticism: ignores social context and individual differences, treats people as machine parts, no flexibility or innovation, cannot cope with uncertainty.
2. The Hawthorne studies (Mayo & Roethlisberger, 1920s): 
	1. raising lighting raised productivity — but so did lowering it back, and the control group improved too. Physical conditions had limited impact; group/peer control played a major role; work and private life interlinked. 
	2. Fundamental points: financial rewards are not the only motivator, and the measuring process affects the performance of what is measured ('what gets measured gets done').
3. McGregor's Theory X vs Theory Y: 
	1. X — average people are lazy, dislike work, need close supervision and comprehensive control and reward from authoritarian managers. 
	2. Y — average people don't dislike work; work may be a source of satisfaction or punishment. People exercise self-control and self-direction. Reward: self-actualisation.

#### Maslow's hierarchy of needs
![[People-slides-Jan-2023.pdf#page=121&rect=28,123,816,506|People-slides-Jan-2023, p.121]]

EXTRA — McClelland's acquired needs: need for achievement, need for affiliation, need for power.
EXTRA — Expectancy theory: MF = V × I × E (Valence × Instrumentality × Expectancy); behaviour depends on whether we perceive it leads to the desired outcome; assumes rational behaviour.

#### Outcome theories
Reinforcement (Skinner): 
Extrinsic motivation: 
- condition behaviour by adding/removing rewarding or aversive stimulus. Adding rewards works better than removing punishments
- aversive feedback is less effective without a way to improve
- too much reinforcement can reduce intrinsic motivation. 
- Take-aways: be specific, be immediate, make targets achievable.
Intrinsic motivation: 
- behaviour driven by internal rewards (positive emotions of the task itself). 
- More resilient to setbacks
- essential when creating new projects. 
- Encourage through tasks building on curiosity, challenge, autonomy, ability to help others.

#### Approaches and tools: Autonomy, Mastery, Purpose
- Autonomy: flexible/trust-based working hours, home office, flat hierarchies, open door, bottom-up kaizen, discretionary funds, responsibility-based planning
- Mastery: specialist career path (not everyone wants to be a manager), communities of practice, mentoring, hiring for attitude and training for skill
- Purpose: clear shared vision of the bigger-picture contribution, value stream mapping, clear 'line of sight' between personal objectives, deliverables, benefits and strategy

#### Motivation in short
- Include the team in planning
- reward and acknowledge
- show interest for the individual
- delegate responsibility
- celebrate achievements
- show your commitment through words and action.

### Leading: How do we guide?

#### Leadership vs management
- Leadership: elusive term. Occurs among people, involves the use of influence, used to attain goals (Daft et al 2010). Obtaining results from others through personal influence.
- Management: applying and administering authority over others; authority is determined by the formal structure of the organisation. "The manager is the dynamic, life-giving element in every business…" (Drucker)
- In PM the terms are often interchanged — excellence requires elements of both.
- Traits approach: 'leaders are born and not made'; skills and attributes are teachable; no single recipe for success (Mullins, 2007).
